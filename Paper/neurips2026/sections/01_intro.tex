\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Unstructured pruning removes individual weights from a trained network and then
retrains what remains \citep{han2015learning}. Up to moderate sparsity the
procedure is close to free: a gradual, schedule-driven magnitude criterion
\citep{zhu2018prune,gale2019state} recovers essentially dense accuracy on
CIFAR-scale image classifiers at 90--95\% of weights removed
\citep{liu2021granet}. Past that point the picture changes. As the surviving
weight budget shrinks toward a few tenths of a percent, retraining stops being a
repair operation and starts being a re-derivation: the network must rebuild a
representation from a support set too small to hold the one it had. The failure
is visible in the published record --- at extreme sparsity, methods that are
within a fraction of a point of each other at 90\% separate by tens of points,
and some collapse to chance \citep{li2025east}.

This suggests a different target for the retraining signal. Cross-entropy asks
the sparse network only to recover the \emph{decisions} of the dense one; it says
nothing about the intermediate representation those decisions are read from, and
at extreme sparsity the representation is what has been destroyed. An objective
that constrains the backbone directly --- that asks the sparse student's
embedding of an image to stay close to the dense model's embedding of that same
image, and away from its embeddings of other classes --- gives the optimiser a
denser, better-conditioned target than a ten-way label.

That objective already exists. \citet{xu2022cap} introduced Contrastive Pruning
(CAP), which supervises a network \emph{during} gradual pruning with three
contrastive terms against three frozen teachers: the pretrained model (PrC), the
task-fine-tuned model (FiC), and the sparse snapshots the run has produced so far
(SnC). CAP was developed and evaluated on pre-trained language models, up to 97\%
sparsity. \textbf{The PrC/FiC/SnC decomposition, and the four-term objective that
combines them with cross-entropy, are CAP's, not ours.} We state this here rather
than in a footnote because it bounds everything that follows.

What we contribute is a controlled study of that objective in a setting CAP did
not examine. We call the resulting configuration \emph{Backbone Contrastive
Pruning} (BaCP): CAP's contrastive supervision applied to convolutional vision
backbones under a gradual static pruning schedule, run against an iterative
pruning (I.P.) baseline that is identical in every respect --- same schedule,
same criterion, same epoch budget, same fine-tune, same data --- except the
training objective. Because the two arms differ only in the objective, whatever
separates them is attributable to the objective and to nothing else. That matched
budget, not any absolute accuracy figure, is the unit of evidence in this paper.

\paragraph{Contributions.} Stated narrowly, and in the order of how much of the
work they represent:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Extension from language to vision backbones.} We port CAP's
  contrastive pruning objective from pre-trained language models to ResNet and
  VGG image classifiers, and report it under a matched-budget comparison against
  an I.P.\ baseline on CIFAR-10.
\item \textbf{Criterion-agnosticism.} The objective is orthogonal to the scoring
  rule that decides which weights die. We evaluate three criteria --- magnitude
  \citep{han2015learning}, SNIP-it \citep{lee2019snip,verdenius2020snipit} and
  WANDA \citep{sun2024wanda} --- under one global threshold, so that the columns
  of our results table differ only in the score, and ask whether the contrastive
  objective helps under all three.
\item \textbf{The extreme-sparsity regime.} We run the grid to 99.9\% sparsity,
  where \citet{xu2022cap} reported 97\% and where the vision pruning literature
  reports its sharpest divergences between methods.
\end{enumerate}

We are also explicit about what this study does not establish. It uses a single
dataset and a single modality; its compute budget is well below that of the
published comparators we quote for context; and the projection head our objective
is measured through is trainable and unpruned, which --- as we argue in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} --- means the measured result supports the claim
\emph{``this objective produces better sparse models''} but not, on the loss
alone, the stronger claim \emph{``this objective preserves representations.''}
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} makes those limits precise.
